# Camera-card production

This records the method Tyler approved, with his later layout correction applied for future clips. It is a production recipe, not a fixed episode batch.

1. Identify the precise camera, generation, and variant from the recording and surrounding discussion. Verify the manufacturer's concise official model name, retaining its exact spelling, capitalization, numerals, suffixes, spaces, and hyphens; include the brand only when needed. Do not use a long marketing/retailer title or infer a generation from a partial name. Match any later older-camera anecdote separately. Obtain an authentic manufacturer product photo and verify model, effective megapixels, lens focal length/aperture, weight basis, and original release year. Keep the supplied product appearance recognizable.
2. Use an image-generation tool to prepare the cutout plate: dark forest-green textured paper, photographic camera with a slightly irregular white die-cut border and soft shadow. Make the camera about 70–75% of the frame width in the lower portion. Leave the top for all specifications and reserve the dialogue-caption band. For a caption center around 82% height, a camera center near 62% can be a starting point, but use the real image bounds and caption width to establish clearance. Do not prescribe a position without checking it.
3. Typeset all model/spec copy at the top in brand off-white. The approved working color was #F4F0E6; prefer a supplied current brand value. Staatliches for the model heading and a clean semibold sans-serif such as Inter for specs matched the approved treatment. Do not distribute specifications below the image. Use short labels and clearly distinguish equivalent focal length and battery/card-inclusive weight.
4. Compose outside Riverside. A local native video renderer worked: AppKit for typography/composition and AVFoundation's H.264 writer for the finished MP4. Use the edit's vertical dimensions/frame rate and keep an editable source plate, copy, and renderer/project. A small entrance zoom settling into a still hold, with brief staggered text reveals, is enough. Count readable hold only after the final reveal. Keep animation paths away from captions.
5. Inspect representative decoded frames and playback of the finished asset. Verify camera/photo fidelity, type, layout, legibility, dimensions, duration, and motion. Do not infer successful video encoding from an output file merely existing; check the encoder result and playable output.
6. Upload that one completed video to Riverside. Replace superseded card/photo/spec layers in the same edit, leaving approved dialogue captions and audio intact. Use an overlay when covering existing dialogue so the story does not grow. Current tool schemas govern media readiness, placement, layer order, source/playable time conversion, and revision handling.
7. Read back the inserted clip's actual bounds. In the observed workflow, a low-level overlay duration expanded across removed material, so intended arguments were not enough to establish the saved duration. Inspect the actual playable result rather than preserving any historical workaround as a universal API contract.
8. Inspect the insert's exit and the next speaker shot frame by frame. A roughly 0.2-second fragment of Gruber reappeared between the Ricoh card and Tyler; holding Tyler's angle from the card exit removed that flash without cutting dialogue. Inspect all other short playable shots too. Preserve intentional reactions.
9. Complete the current export through the supported workflow and verify playback in Riverside's Clips feed, not only its editor. In the observed workflow, the image-only layer appeared on the desktop canvas but was absent in mobile Clips; the externally rendered video insert survived feed playback. Completed API exports alone did not establish that the app-facing preview was updated. Review the correct revision and distinguish completed render status from verified feed playback.

Keep temporary captures and diagnostic frames in task work storage. Follow the main skill's review-in-app and preview-cleanup rules; preserve final media and editable source assets.
